Over-Terrain Vehicle

Mechanical Design

Our data team built an over-terrain vehicle for a simulated hazardous-payload recovery mission. The assignment combined three tasks on one chassis: navigate to a data pylon, read its electrical signal, and handle a physical sample.

Goal

Build a rover for a simulated payload recovery mission. It had to read a signal at the pylon, remove and identify a puck, clear three obstacles, and finish entirely inside the destination zone.

Solution & Results

Our team built a four-wheel prototype with onboard electronics, integrated wifi, with an ArUco marker. The CAD assembly shows how a vertical linear actuator fits beside the deck.

What I learned

I learned to compare routes before committing to a mechanism. Choosing larger tires for the bump changed what we needed from the arm. We could keep it fixed and spend less of our weight budget on motors and linkages.

Design constraints

Our team had to choose between driving under the limbo bar and climbing over a bump. We initially considered a folding arm that could lie flat to clear the bar. With a vehicle weight limit, the motors and linkages required to fold the arm would have made that design too heavy. We chose the bump route and increased the tire diameter so the rover could climb the obstacle. That choice also let us use a fixed arm and eliminate the additional motor and folding linkage.

From CAD to the arena

The CAD model shows how the linear actuator fits at the edge of the chassis. An upright bracket holds its body above the deck, with the rod extending down through the cutout. The arena photo shows the four treaded wheels, exposed control electronics, and raised marker mast on our prototype.

Reading the payload signal

The vehicle had to approach within 150 mm of the payload and connect to two aluminum pads inside the pylon. The upper pad provided ground and the lower pad carried a 100 Hz square wave. Each cycle lasts 10 ms, so a 7 ms high pulse corresponds to a 70% duty cycle. The mission required the vehicle to transmit the measured duty cycle within 5% of the assigned value.

Reaching and identifying the puck

The 50 mm diameter, 13 mm tall puck sat at the bottom of a roughly 136 mm tall pylon. Its 76.2 mm inner diameter leaves about 13 mm of radial clearance around a centered puck. The mission called for complete removal and a report of whether the puck was magnetic. Both versions had a plastic exterior, but the magnetic version contained five embedded magnets.

Finishing the course

The duty cycle and puck type could change between runs, so the vehicle had to determine both during the mission. To finish, the entire vehicle had to pass all three obstacles and enter the destination zone. These were the course acceptance criteria.

3D-Printed Phone Holder

Mechanical Design, FEA

I designed and printed a desktop phone stand in SolidWorks. The challenge was to remove material while keeping the phone stable and leaving room for its charging cable.

Goal

Design and print a lightweight desktop stand that supports a phone in portrait and landscape while leaving the charging port accessible.

Solution & Results

I opened up the body and kept two continuous curved supports. I checked the revised geometry in SolidWorks and tested the printed stand with real devices.

What I learned

I learned to check stiffness and stability separately. SolidWorks helped me estimate how far the hooks would flex after I removed material. Putting my phone in the printed stand showed me whether it stayed balanced in portrait and landscape and whether I could still plug in the charging cable.

Modeling the stand in SolidWorks

The side profile sets the viewing angle. Two front hooks hold the phone in place, with a gap between them for the charging cable. The CAD views and dimensioned drawing show how those features fit into a single printed part.

Design challenges

My first version weighed nearly 100 g because I had made the stand larger for stability. The challenge was deciding where I could remove material without weakening the supports under the phone. I cut openings in the body and kept the curved sides continuous from the front hooks to the base. That left two questions to check: how much the supports would flex under load, and whether the lighter stand would stay upright on the desk. I used SolidWorks for the first check and the printed prototype for the second.

Checking stress and deflection

The simulation predicted a maximum von Mises stress of 4.352 MPa. Dividing the modeled yield strength of 30 MPa by that stress gives a safety factor of about 6.9 against yielding for this load case. The displacement plot shows a peak of 0.09057 cm, or about 0.91 mm.

Testing the printed stand

I checked the printed stand with my phone in both orientations and demonstrated charging. I also tried a 464 g iPad Air, and the stand stayed upright. That was a hands-on stability check with one device.

Robotic Arm Assembly

Assemblies, Mechanical Design

For ENME 272, I modeled an articulated robot arm as an assembly and documented it in two engineering drawings.

Goal

Model an articulated arm for a pick-and-place task: reach a part, orient the gripper around it, and move it to a second position. The upper and lower links provide reach, while the wrist sets the angle of approach.

Challenges

Reaching a part is only useful if the jaws can approach it from the right angle. The arm links and wrist have to work together to give the gripper clearance as it approaches and withdraws. At the wrist, several components overlap in the assembled view, so I used enlarged details and an exploded drawing to show how the gripper fits together.

Solution & Results

I used enlarged detail views to show the gripper and joint geometry. The exploded drawing pairs numbered callouts with a bill of materials listing eight distinct items and nine components in total, including two matching gripper jaws.

What I learned

I learned to think about the gripper’s approach as well as the arm’s reach. The wrist needs room to turn the jaws toward a part. Making an exploded drawing also taught me how to show where the two matching jaws fit when their mounting details overlap in the assembled view.

Active DRS Rear Wing

Mechanical Design, Aerodynamics, Prototyping

My work on the Terps EV rear wing involves aerodynamic testing and coordination with the front wing and liquids teams. The design challenge is balancing downforce and drag while fitting the wing into the vehicle as a whole.

Goal

Develop an active drag reduction system (DRS) for the Terps EV rear wing so the car can use different aerodynamic configurations for cornering and straight-line driving.

Challenges

A wing configuration that produces downforce in a corner also creates drag on a straight. The rear wing also has to fit the car and work with the front wing and cooling layout.

Solution & Results

Our team is developing a movable rear-wing flap. My work focuses on aerodynamic testing and coordinating design decisions with the front wing and liquids teams.

What I learned

Working with Terps EV has changed how I think about downforce. A flap position that helps in a corner can cost speed on a straight. I’m learning to weigh that tradeoff alongside the front wing’s effect on the airflow and the cooling team’s requirements.

F1 Car Body

Mechanical Design, Aerodynamics, Prototyping

I modeled an F1-style car body in SolidWorks. A narrow nose rises into the wider center section, with sidepods along the flanks and a rear wing mounted above the tapered tail.

Goal

My goal was to design an F1-style car body that could generate downforce for cornering while limiting drag on the straights. I approached the nose, wings, sidepods, and tail as parts of one aerodynamic system. At the front, I wanted the nose and wing supports to leave a clear path for air beneath the nose and around the body. From there, my focus was on shaping the transition from the wider sidepods to the narrower tail and positioning the rear wing above it. The CAD model was intended to provide a baseline for future airflow analysis, particularly how changes to the body contours and wing placement affect drag and downforce.

Challenges

At the front, the nose curves down close to the wing, leaving little space to show the support between them. At the rear, the body tapers just where the upright needs to meet the wing.

Solution & Results

The full-body view shows the proportions from nose to tail. The detail views show the curved support beneath the nose and the upright connecting the rear wing to the tail.

What I learned

I learned to check a model at two scales. The full-body view helped me judge the proportions, while a close side view made the gap between the nose and front wing much easier to see.

Wood Truss Competition

Structural Design

Our team is developing a symmetrical truss for a destructive load test. The design uses 0.75 × 0.75 in square members and 0.118 in gusset plates. Our team describes the stock as pine oak wood. The current CAD drawing specifies pine wood and lists a modeled weight of 0.57 lb.

Goal

Carry as much load as possible for the weight of the truss and predict where failure will begin. Before testing, we will identify a specific member or joint, name its failure mode, and predict the applied load that would cause it to fail.

Challenges

The truss must span a 22 in gap, and the rules require an overall length greater than 22 in and less than 23 in. At each member end, glue contact can cover at most 15% of the member length. That limits joint area while leaving at least 70% of the member unsupported by gussets.

Solution & Results

The current SolidWorks drawing defines the member layout and plate geometry. Next we will solve for support reactions and axial forces in terms of the applied load, then compare the loads that would cause wood rupture or crushing, compression-member buckling, and glue-line shear. The lowest predicted load will determine which location we mark on the drawing. Physical load-test results are pending.

What I learned

I’m learning to look beyond the member carrying the largest force. A long diagonal can buckle under compression, while a shorter member can transfer enough force through a small glue area to make its joint the weak point. Comparing both limits at the same applied load gives our team a prediction we can check during testing.

Geometry and joint layout

The front view shows the 7.00 in height, paired 9.53 in outer diagonal dimensions, and mirrored inner panels. The top and end views show the square stock and the gusset plates on either side of the joints.

Competition scoring and load test

The rubric assigns 70 points to the failure calculations and 50 points to the competition score sheet. Judges assess construction quality, aesthetics, theme, teamwork, and predictions of failure location, mode, and load. During testing, the load is applied at the top center until the truss breaks or reaches the 3,000 lb test limit. Faculty record the truss weight and maximum load to evaluate strength-to-weight ratio.

Wind Turbine Assembly

Mechanical Design, Assemblies

This CAD project brings individual wind turbine components into one SolidWorks assembly. It gave me practice defining mates and thinking through how the rotor, housing, and support relate to one another.

Goal

Build a SolidWorks assembly that shows how the rotor, housing, and support fit together.

Challenges

The rotor axis has to line up with the housing. Each mate affects how the parts can move, so the choice of reference faces and axes matters.

Solution & Results

I brought the individual components together in SolidWorks and used assembly mates to define their relative positions. The resulting model shows the rotor, housing, and support together in one assembly.

What I learned

I learned to decide which movements an assembly needs before choosing its mates. For the turbine, that meant thinking separately about what centers the rotor on the housing and what lets it turn about that axis.

SolidWorks model and drawings

Section A–A shows how the shaft and bearings fit inside the housing. The exploded view identifies the parts and their quantities, including two bearings and three blades.
